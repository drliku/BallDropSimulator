Why does a bell curve appear?

1

Every peg is a coin flip

At each of the 12 rows a ball bounces right with probability p = 0.50 or left with probability 1 − p = 0.50. The bounces are independent, and the bin a ball lands in is simply the number of rightward bounces, k.

2

Counting paths gives the binomial

Every path with exactly k rights has probability pk(1−p)n−k, and there are C(n, k) such paths — Pascal’s triangle hidden in the pegs. So

P(k) = C(n, k) · pk · (1 − p)n − k

with mean np and standard deviation √(np(1 − p)). Middle bins are reachable by far more paths than the edges, which is why they fill fastest.

3

More rows → the normal curve

The landing bin is a sum of many small independent ±½ steps. By the de Moivre–Laplace theorem (a special case of the central limit theorem), as rows increase the binomial shape approaches a normal curve N(np, np(1−p)). With p = 50% it is symmetric from the start; skewed choices need more rows before the bell takes shape. Turn on Normal approx. and slide the rows from 5 to 20 to watch the two curves converge.

4

Randomness, then regularity

A single ball is unpredictable — drop one and follow its path. But the law of large numbers makes the histogram hug the theoretical curve ever more closely: relative errors shrink roughly like 1/√N. The χ² test in the statistics panel tells you whether any remaining gap is just chance.

Paths are generated by an exact random walk (one independent Bernoulli trial per row), then animated — so the bins follow the binomial distribution precisely.
